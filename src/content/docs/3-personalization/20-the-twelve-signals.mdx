A shopper group's priorities are a weighted mix of twelve signals. You choose which ones matter and how much — that mix is the rule, and it is yours to read and change. This page is what each signal actually measures, and what it needs before it does anything.

## The twelve

| Signal | What it boosts |
| --- | --- |
| **Recently viewed** | Products the visitor browsed in this session or recent ones |
| **Favorite category affinity** | Products whose category or attributes match the visitor's profile |
| **Cart history** | Products currently in the visitor's cart |
| **Repeat purchase** | Products the visitor bought before — for reorder flows |
| **Top sellers** | Shop-wide bestsellers, inside the collection's window |
| **New arrivals** | Products released in roughly the last 30 days |
| **Session intent** | Products similar to what the visitor clicked in this visit |
| **Return rate penalty** | *Lowers* products with a high historical return rate |
| **Price affinity** | Products near the price band this visitor browses or buys in, fading as the price moves away |
| **Intent match** | Products whose intent tags match what the shopper is doing right now — treating themselves, buying a gift, replacing something, browsing, or close to deciding |
| **Bought together** | Products your customers buy together with what is in the visitor's cart or what they just viewed, more often than chance |
| **Trending** | Products selling faster over the last 30 days than over the 30 before |

## Most of these work on a stranger

This is the part worth internalising, because it is the opposite of how recommendation engines usually work: **ten of the twelve need no purchase history and no login.**

*Top sellers*, *New arrivals*, *Trending* and *Return rate penalty* are facts about your catalogue — they apply to anyone. *Recently viewed*, *Session intent*, *Cart history*, *Price affinity* and *Bought together* are read from the visit happening right now, so they start working within a few pageviews. *Favorite category affinity* builds from browsing.

Only **Repeat purchase** genuinely requires a shopper who has bought before.

<Callout type="tip" title="This is what makes a first-visit rule possible">
A group defined as first-time visitors, weighted toward Top sellers, New arrivals and Session intent, is a complete and useful rule — no identity, no history, no quiz. Most stores' collection traffic is people you have never seen before, so this is usually the group worth writing first.
</Callout>

## Two signals that read your orders, not the shopper

**Bought together** and **Trending** are *context* signals: they say something about your store rather than about the person. Bought together counts a pair once it has been bought together in at least two orders, and lifts those partners when one half is in the cart or was just viewed. Trending needs a product to have sold at least three units in the last 30 days before its rate of change counts. Neither is a model feature — they are arithmetic over your orders — so they start working as soon as the order import has finished, with no training.

## The ones that need something first

Three signals will sit in your mix scoring nothing at all unless a condition is met, and none of them announces it:

**Repeat purchase** only applies to products marked **replenishable** — consumables you would expect somebody to buy again. That is deliberate: re-surfacing a mattress to the person who just bought one is noise. But the flag is a product metafield, and it is **not filled in for you**. Until something sets it, this signal returns nothing for every product.

**Favorite category affinity** can only describe products that carry taxonomy values. On a shop where **Fill product values with AI** (Settings → Product data & AI) has not run, most products have no category to match on and the signal stays near zero.

**Intent match** needs Intent AI enabled in Settings, and the plan that carries it — Growth applies it live; Pro reads in shadow and reports. Weighted without that, it contributes nothing.

<Callout type="note" title="A signal that needs data is not a broken signal">
All three behave the same way when their condition is missing: they score zero and let the rest of your mix decide. The editor marks them as having no data rather than hiding it — a weight you thought was doing work is not, which is worth knowing before you conclude a rule underperforms.
</Callout>

## Weighting them

Give more weight to what should decide more. There is no correct set — a fashion store leaning on *New arrivals* and a supplement store leaning on *Repeat purchase* are both right, because they sell differently.

Two habits that help: start with two or three signals rather than all twelve, and change one thing at a time so you can tell what moved.
