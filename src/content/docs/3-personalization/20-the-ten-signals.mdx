A shopper group's ranking is a weighted mix of ten signals. You choose which ones matter and how much — that mix is the rule, and it is yours to read and change. This page is what each signal actually measures, and what it needs before it does anything.

## The ten

| Signal | What it boosts |
| --- | --- |
| **Recently viewed** | Products the visitor browsed in this session or recent ones |
| **Favorite category affinity** | Products whose category or attributes match the visitor's profile |
| **Cart history** | Products the visitor has added to cart, now or before |
| **Repeat purchase** | Products the visitor bought before — for reorder flows |
| **Top sellers** | Shop-wide bestsellers, inside the collection's window |
| **New arrivals** | Products released in roughly the last 30 days |
| **Session intent** | Products similar to what the visitor clicked in this visit |
| **Return rate penalty** | *Lowers* products with a high historical return rate |
| **Price affinity** | Products near the price band this visitor browses or buys in, fading as the price moves away |
| **Emotional affinity** | Products whose tags match the shopper's read state |

## Most of these work on a stranger

This is the part worth internalising, because it is the opposite of how recommendation engines usually work: **eight of the ten need no purchase history and no login.**

*Top sellers*, *New arrivals* and *Return rate penalty* are facts about your catalogue — they apply to anyone. *Recently viewed*, *Session intent*, *Cart history* and *Price affinity* are read from the visit happening right now, so they start working within a few pageviews. *Favorite category affinity* builds from browsing.

Only **Repeat purchase** genuinely requires a shopper who has bought before.

<Callout type="tip" title="This is what makes a first-visit rule possible">
A group defined as first-time visitors, weighted toward Top sellers, New arrivals and Session intent, is a complete and useful rule — no identity, no history, no quiz. Most stores' collection traffic is people you have never seen before, so this is usually the group worth writing first.
</Callout>

## The two that need something first

Two signals will sit in your mix scoring nothing at all unless a condition is met, and neither announces it:

**Repeat purchase** only applies to products marked **replenishable** — consumables you would expect somebody to buy again. That is deliberate: re-surfacing a mattress to the person who just bought one is noise. But the flag is a product metafield, and it is **not filled in for you**. Until something sets it, this signal returns nothing for every product. If you sell consumables and you weight it, set the flag first.

**Emotional affinity** needs Emotional Suggest switched on in Settings, and the plan that carries it. Weighted without that, it contributes nothing.

<Callout type="note" title="A signal that needs data is not a broken signal">
Both behave the same way when their condition is missing: they score zero and let the rest of your mix decide. Nothing breaks — but a weight you thought was doing work is not, which is worth knowing before you conclude a rule underperforms.
</Callout>

## Weighting them

Give more weight to what should decide more. There is no correct set — a fashion store leaning on *New arrivals* and a supplement store leaning on *Repeat purchase* are both right, because they sell differently.

Two habits that help: start with two or three signals rather than all ten, and change one thing at a time so you can tell what moved.
